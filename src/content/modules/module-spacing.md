# Interlude — 띄어쓰기: The Space Bar Is Grammar

A five-minute reading, no vocabulary to memorize. You've been absorbing Korean spacing by osmosis for four modules; this makes the rules you already half-know explicit — and tells you why the space bar is the site of Korea's favorite grammar joke.

---

## The joke every Korean knows

> **아버지가방에들어가신다**

Written without spaces, this sentence is ambiguous between two readings:

- 아버지**가 방에** 들어가신다 — *Father enters the room.* (아버지 + subject 가, 방 "room" + 에)
- 아버지 **가방에** 들어가신다 — *Father enters the bag.* (가방 "bag" + 에)

Same letters, one space apart: a respectful sentence about Dad, or Dad climbing into luggage. This is the national example of why 띄어쓰기 (ttuieosseugi — "spacing," literally *writing-with-gaps*) matters.

---

## The rules you already follow

Korean uses spaces — unlike Japanese or Chinese — but the space falls in different places than English. The master rule: **grammar glues, words breathe.**

**1. Particles glue to their noun.** You've done this since Module 1: 저**는**, 학교**에서**, 커피**를**, 친구**하고**. A particle is never an island — writing 저 는 is as wrong as writing "book s" in English.

**2. Endings glue to their verb stem.** 먹 + 었어요 = 먹었어요, one block of text. The honorific 시, the past 았/었, the 요 — all welded on.

**3. Helper constructions get a space.** 먹**고 싶어요** (want to eat), 안 **먹어요** (the negation adverb 안 stands alone). When two verb-ish pieces cooperate, they usually keep a polite distance.

**4. Numbers and their counters are spaced.** 커피 두 잔, 세 시 — the number and its measure word each get room. (Module 5, next, is all about these.)

**5. Names breathe before their titles.** 민수 씨, 김 선생님 — the 씨 and the title stand off from the name.

**Compound words weld solid** when they've fused into one concept: 월요일 (Monday), 학교 (school), 배고프다 (to be hungry — literally "stomach-empty," but it's one word now).

The comforting truth: **spacing is the part of Korean orthography natives get wrong most often.** Job applications are proofread for 띄어쓰기 the way English résumés are proofread for its/it's. Your mistakes here will be in good company.

## Punctuation: mostly imported, a few local customs

Modern Korean punctuation is basically Western — periods, commas, question marks, exclamation points, all doing what you expect. The local flavor:

- **Questions really need the ?** — you learned in Module 1 that a -요 question is *identical* to a statement. In writing, the question mark does all the work: 커피 좋아해요. / 커피 좋아해요?
- **The tilde ~** marks ranges (1~5) — and in messages it stretches sounds for warmth: 네~ ("suuure"), 안녕~. It's tone of voice, typed.
- **The interpunct ·** joins listed items: 김치·불고기·비빔밥. (You've seen it in this app's headers since day one.)
- **Quotes:** modern text uses “ ” and ‘ ’; older or vertical typesetting used 「 」 and 『 』 — you'll meet those in book titles.
- **No space before punctuation, one after** — same as English.

---

## Why the spaces are only ~130 years old

Traditional Korean was written top-to-bottom, right-to-left, with **no spaces at all** — as Chinese still is. Spacing arrived with the first Hangul-only newspaper, the 독립신문 (*The Independent*, 1896), which needed ordinary readers to parse lines fast. The Korean Language Society standardized the rules in its 1933 orthography plan — the same reform that settled modern spelling. So spacing isn't ancient tradition; it's a modernization that won because it *works*, which is also why the father-and-the-bag joke lands: everyone remembers what sentences look like without it.
