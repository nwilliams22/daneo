# Korean, Word-First — Module 9: Requests, Offers, and Shopping

Everything so far has been *describing* the world. This module is where you start **operating** it: asking for things, offering things, proposing plans, and paying. The center of gravity is the single most useful conjugation in the language — **주세요** — and by the end of the module you can walk into any shop, cafe, or restaurant in Korea and leave with what you wanted.

**How to use this:** These sentences are scripts. Say them out loud in full until the 주세요-frame is muscle memory — this is the module you'll actually use on day one in Seoul.

---

## Part 1 — The transaction, as theater

Every Korean shop visit runs the same beats, and now you know your lines:

> Door: **어서 오세요!** (welcome — you say nothing; maybe nod)
> You, browsing: **저기요 / 여기요** — excuse me! · **얼마예요?** — how much?
> Owner: **오천 원이에요.**
> You: **이거...** *(pointing works)* **주세요. 포장해 주세요.**
> Owner: **봉투 드릴까요?** — shall I give you a bag?
> You: **네 / 괜찮아요.**
> Both: **감사합니다!**

The cast: **사장님** (the owner — address them this way and doors open), **손님** (you, the honored customer). Korean service culture is fast, warm, and runs entirely on the machinery in this module.

## Part 3 — The glue

### 1. The 주세요 machine

주다 (give) + -(으)세요 = **주세요** — *please give me*. Three settings:

- **[noun] 주세요** — 물 주세요, 커피 주세요
- **[noun] [number] [counter] 주세요** — 김밥 두 개 주세요 (Module 5 docks right in)
- **[verb]-아/어 주세요** — *do it for me*: 도와**주세요** (help me), 보여 **주세요** (show me), 바꿔 주세요 (exchange it), 계산해 주세요 (check, please)

Soften any of them with **좀**: 물 **좀** 주세요 — it means "just/could you," not "a small amount." (Gap deck.)

### 2. -(으)세요 — the polite imperative

You met this ending in Module 4 honoring *subjects* (선생님이 가세요 — the teacher goes). Aimed at *you*, the same ending is a polite request: 앉으**세요** (please sit), 여기 오세요 (come here). Same syllables, two jobs — context decides, and the gap deck sorts it. This is also the skeleton key to goodbyes you already half-know: 안녕히 가세요 = "please go peacefully."

### 3. Offers and proposals

- **-(으)ㄹ까요?** — *shall we/I?*: 같이 먹을**까요**? (shall we eat together?) 드릴**까요**? (shall I give you…? — cashier speech)
- **-(으)ㅂ시다** — *let's*, with rally-the-team starch: 갑**시다**! Among friends, soft 같이 가요 does the same job.

### 4. Big-number money

Prices are Sino-Korean, stacked in **만-units** (ten-thousands, not thousands):

| ₩ | Korean |
|---|--------|
| 500 | 오백 원 |
| 1,000 | 천 원 *(no 일)* |
| 5,000 | 오천 원 |
| 10,000 | **만 원** *(never 일만)* |
| 35,000 | 삼만 오천 원 |

얼마예요? → read the answer in 만/천/백. Two weeks of prices and it's automatic.

## Part 5 — Practice

Cover the right column and translate; then flip and build the Korean.

1. Water, please. → 물 주세요.
2. Two gimbap, please. → 김밥 두 개 주세요.
3. How much is it? → 얼마예요?
4. It's 5,000 won. → 오천 원이에요.
5. Please sit here. → 여기 앉으세요.
6. To go, please. → 포장해 주세요.
7. Shall we have lunch together? → 같이 점심을 먹을까요?
8. More, please. → 더 주세요.

Price drill: read these out loud — 이천 원 · 팔천 원 · 만 원 · 사만 오천 원. Then say what you'd buy with each.
