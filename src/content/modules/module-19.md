# Korean, Word-First — Module 19: Friends and Casual Speech

Eighteen modules of 존댓말 — polite speech — and every sentence wore its 요 like a pressed collar. Today the collar comes off. **반말** — half-speech, the register of friends, siblings, and anyone you've earned it with — is mostly a *removal*: you already know every form in this module. You're just learning what Korean sounds like relaxed.

> **Warm-up (Module 18):** build these out loud — Module 18 blocks, combinations you've never seen written: *Please recommend a stew* (찌개를 추천해 주세요) · *The soup is a little bland* (국이 좀 싱거워요) · *Let's order delivery* (배달 시킵시다). If the blocks don't snap together, visit Review first.

**How to use this:** every sentence in this module is in 반말 — the whole set is the demonstration. But read Part 3's WHO rules first: the grammar is trivial; the sociology is everything. This module completes the register ladder Module 4 started.

---

## Part 1 — Who gets 반말 (read this before speaking it)

The mechanics take five minutes; knowing when takes a childhood. The rules:

- **Same-age friends** — and the gap deck has news about what 친구 really means: a *same-age* peer. This is why Koreans ask your age immediately — they're calibrating this exact choice.
- **Younger people you're close to**, kids, younger family.
- **Never first** with elders, strangers, coworkers — remember M4's gap item: 말 놓으세요, "put the speech down," is the *invitation*, and it isn't yours to issue upward.
- When unsure: 존댓말. Nobody was ever offended by too much politeness; the reverse ends friendships before they start.

동갑이에요? Same age? Then somebody says 말 놓자 — let's drop the speech — and everything below unlocks.

## Part 2 — The bricks

The pronouns politeness was hiding, the friendship nouns, the falling-out-and-making-up verbs.

::vocab::

**The pronoun swap:** 저/제 (humble) step aside for **나/내** (plain — 나 has been in your checklist since M1, waiting). New: **너** (you) and **네** (your) — except 네 sounds exactly like 내 AND like yes-네, so real speech says **니**. Written one way, spoken another; the interlude's oldest lesson.

## Part 3 — The glue

### 1. Drop the 요 — that's most of it

- 가요 → **가** · 먹어요 → **먹어** · 있어요 → **있어** · 괜찮아요 → **괜찮아**
- Questions: same words, rising pitch. 뭐 해? 어디야? 화났어?
- Commands: the bare -아/어 form. 와! (come!) 웃어! (laugh!)
- Prohibition: -지 마세요 → **-지 마**. (M14's machine, shorter lever.)

### 2. The copula gets a new casual face

이에요/예요 → **이야/야**: 비밀이야, 농담이야, 최고야. And 아니에요 → **아니야**. This is the one genuinely new form — everything else was subtraction.

### 3. -자 — let's

반말's own propositive: 가**자**! 먹**자**! 놀**자**! M9's -(으)ㅂ시다 with the tie off. Attach to the bare stem; even ㄹ-verbs behave (놀자).

### 4. Names get vocatives

Consonant-ending names take **아** (지민아!), vowel-ending take **야** (민수야!). The sound your name makes when a Korean friend actually calls you — the small music of belonging.

### The register ladder, complete

| formal | polite | casual |
|---|---|---|
| 안녕하세요 | 안녕하세요 | **안녕** |
| 감사합니다 | 고마워요 | **고마워** |
| 죄송합니다 | 미안해요 | **미안해** |
| 축하합니다 | 축하해요 | **축하해** |

Module 4 opened this door; this module walks through it.

## Part 4 — Building sentences

**Spot the recycling:** 떡볶이 먹으러 가자 sends M18's street food through M12's -(으)러 into the new -자, and 오늘 저녁에 외식하자 is Module 18's 외식해요 plan, re-registered between friends — the same happy idea, one collar looser.

::sentences::

**Text-message bingo:** 뭐 해? · ㅋㅋㅋ (laughter, typed) · 어디야? · 연락해! — you now read 90% of a Korean group chat. The other 10% is emoji and 눈치.

## Part 5 — Practice

Cover the right column and translate; then flip and build the Korean.

1. Hey! Long time no see! → 안녕! 오랜만이야!
2. What are you doing? → 뭐 해?
3. Let's go eat tteokbokki. → 떡볶이 먹으러 가자.
4. Happy birthday! → 생일 축하해!
5. I'm sorry. Are you mad? → 미안해. 화났어?
6. No, it's fine. → 아니야, 괜찮아.
7. Don't lie! → 거짓말하지 마!
8. Let's hang out together this weekend. → 주말에 같이 놀자.

Register drill: take any five polite sentences you know and strip them to 반말 — then say who you could actually say each one to. The grammar is the easy half; the WHO is the fluency.
