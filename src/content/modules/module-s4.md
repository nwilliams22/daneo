# Korean, Word-First — Appendix S4: 사투리 — Korean, Bent by Region

M33 handed you the word 사투리 with a warning label: this app teaches 표준어 — standard Seoul speech, *which no grandmother anywhere speaks*. This appendix pays that sentence off. The official definition of 표준어 is itself a confession: "the modern Seoul speech widely used by cultivated people" — a dialect that got a government job in 1933 and never gave it back. Everyone else kept talking the way their province talks, and half of Korean television is built on the difference.

Here is the good news, and the reason this is an appendix and not a panic: **사투리 is not new vocabulary.** It is your vocabulary, run through regional machines — vowels pressed, endings swapped, pitch restored — the same kind of systematic converters the Konglish reading gave you for loanwords and the 준말 reading gave you for speed. Learn the machines and the dialects decode themselves.

**How to use this:** recognition, not production. A learner performing 사투리 lands the way a tourist performing a Texas drawl lands — the locals will be delighted and you will not be taken seriously. Drill these cards so your EAR knows them: the drama, the market, and your friend's grandmother are the exam. The one exception where production is safe: answering warmth with warmth (응, 뭇다 to a 밥 뭇나? will make someone's day).

## Part 1 — The map you already own

M31 taught you the provinces as two-city welds (전라도 = 전주 + 나주, 경상도 = 경주 + 상주…) and its gap deck filed 팔도 — the eight provinces as the traditional frame for "everywhere." Dialects follow that same map, in two big families and three characters:

- **경상도** (부산, 대구): loud, fast, chopped — the one Koreans call 무뚝뚝 (blunt). Stars in gangster films and buddy comedies.
- **전라도** (광주, 전주): melodic, warm, trailing — the food province's speech, all hospitality and 잉.
- **충청도**: unhurried and famously indirect — the punchline province, and in on the joke.
- **강원도**: the mountain flavor — one ending (-드래요) and a potato reputation (M40's 감자, worn proudly).
- **제주도**: not an accent. Almost another language — UNESCO lists 제주어 as critically endangered.

## Part 2 — The 경상도 machine

The southeast kept two pieces of grammar the capital threw away:

**1. Pitch.** Middle Korean had tones; Seoul flattened them; 경상도 didn't. That's why the region can say 가가 가가? — four identical syllables — and mean "is that kid THAT kid?": the melody carries what the spelling can't (the gap deck files the full party trick). It's also why the accent survives decades in Seoul: you can swap your endings, but your pitch is installed in childhood.

**2. The question fork.** Standard Korean asks every question with -아/어? or -까?. 경상도 splits them the way old Korean did:

| Question type | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Yes/no | **-나?** (past: -았/었나) | 밥 뭇나? = did you eat? |
| With a question word (M10's 뭐/어디/왜 kit) | **-노?** | 뭐 하노? = what are you doing? |
| Yes/no, noun-flavored | **-가?** | 니가 학생이가? |
| Question-word, noun-flavored | **-고?** | 이게 뭐고? |

Hear the ending, know the question type — a distinction 표준어 gave up. The rest of the machine, quickly: ㅆ softens toward ㅅ (쌀 → 살, the stereotype every comedy sketch reaches for), ㅡ and ㅓ drift together, 하다 hardens to 카다 (뭐라고 하노 → 머라카노), statements soften with -데이 (알았데이 = got it, warmly), and politeness runs on -예 instead of -요 (맞아예; 어데예 as the humble wave-off). The icon set — 마, 아이가, 단디, 억수로, 할매, 아지매 — is in your cards, and the region's most famous export is in the gap deck wearing a lei: 니가 가라, 하와이.

## Part 3 — The 전라도 machine

The southwest sings where the southeast chops. The tails give it away: **-잉** hangs warmth on anything (알았어잉 = got iiit~, 잘 가잉 = safe travels, dear), and **-부러** intensifies verbs the way the -아/어 버리다 glue (M52) finalizes them — 죽어부러! = completely, utterly. The two famous citizens:

- **거시기** — the universal placeholder, a word that means whatever both speakers already know it means. A 전라도 conversation can run on remarkably few actual nouns; 거시기 is a trust exercise between people who share context, which is the region's whole social style in one word.
- **겁나** — fear (겁) worn down into "crazy-": 겁나 맛있다. Variety shows carried it out of the province; your group chat probably already has it.

And 아따 — the all-purpose exclamation, one syllable of costume that tells you exactly where a drama character's grandmother lives. Casting directors lean on this machine hard: M33's 사투리 note already told you 전라도 speech stars in food shows — warmth sells 백반, and the region invented the spread.

## Part 4 — The 충청도 machine

The stereotype: slow. The evidence: the national joke in the gap deck, where a warning about a rolling boulder (돌 굴러가유~) arrives after the boulder does. The truth is better than the stereotype: 충청도's real signature is **indirection as courtesy**. The endings stretch (-요 → **-유**: 괜찮아유; -거야 → **-겨**: 뭐라는겨), the tempo relaxes, and the meaning politely declines to commit — 괜찮아유 can be yes, no, or ask-me-tomorrow, and the region considers the ambiguity a kindness: nobody is ever cornered. Comedians from 충청도 have turned this into a national art form, always deadpan, always one beat later than you expect — the delay IS the punchline.

---

## Part 5 — The 강원도 flavor

One ending does most of the work: **-드래요** — a folksy it-turns-out/they-say (M32's -다고 해요 machinery in mountain dress): 결혼했드래요. Dramas hand it to any character from the east coast as a complete biography. The rest of the stereotype is agricultural — 감자 (M40), worn with pride — and the accent is otherwise the gentlest gradient on the map: Seoul ears often can't place it at all, which is its own kind of camouflage.

## Part 6 — 제주: the one that's a language

Everything above is Korean, bent. 제주어 is different in kind: centuries of island isolation kept vocabulary and grammar the mainland lost entirely, and a mainlander needs subtitles — which Korean broadcasters actually provide. UNESCO lists it as **critically endangered** (2010); its fluent speakers are mostly past seventy, and the island now teaches it to schoolchildren as heritage.

What a visitor meets: **혼저옵서예** (welcome — the island's 어서 오세요, on the airport wall and every 흑돼지 restaurant door), **하르방/할망** (grandfather/grandmother — 돌하르방, the stone grandfathers carved from volcanic rock, M22's 돌, guard every gate on the island), and endings that replace the -ㅂ니다 machinery wholesale: -수다 for statements, -꽈? for questions, **감수광?** = are you leaving? — a word a 1977 ballad taught the whole mainland to sing. Learn these three cards and you have, honestly, more 제주어 than most tourists — and a reason to be gentle with the rest: you are hearing a language in its last generation of native speakers.

## Part 7 — The export pipeline

Regional slang doesn't stay regional anymore — media nationalizes it. 겁나 rode variety shows out of 전라도; 아이가 and 마 rode gangster films and 응답하라 1997 out of 부산; the 응답하라 series built an entire franchise on a dorm full of accents. The pattern to notice: Korea imports its TOUGHNESS from 경상도 (every second film gangster), its WARMTH from 전라도 (every food show), its COMEDY from 충청도 (every deadpan) — casting shorthand M33's 사투리 note flagged, running nightly. When a stray regional word lands in your feed, S1's refresh loop already told you what to do: look it up in Explore, save it to the deck. This appendix is the map; the deck is the living document.

## Part 8 — Everyone has an accent

Two truths to leave with. First: 서울말 is also a dialect — the one that got the job. It has its own melody (that long rising 어~?), its own shibboleths, and its speakers are famously the last to know. Second: the stigma is inverting. For decades, arriving in Seoul meant sanding your speech flat — comedians played 사투리 for laughs, announcers trained it away. Now regional idols keep their accents on camera, 부산 flavor sells everything from startups (단디!) to dramas, and speaking your hometown is increasingly worn the way M45 taught 답다 works: being true to what you are. Your ears should be ready for all eight provinces. Your mouth can stay in Seoul.
