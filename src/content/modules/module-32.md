# Korean, Word-First — Module 32: Society and the News

Module 31 gave you the map; this module turns on the television. The vocabulary is the B-list's abstract core — the words news anchors, textbooks, and arguments are made of — and the glue is the machine Ring 2 is famous for: **-다고 하다**, reported speech. By the end of this module you can repeat what anyone said, ask what anything is called, and pass along a rumor with clean hands.

**How to use this:** the abstract words look intimidating and are not — they are two-root hanja compounds, and you own most of the roots. Guess before you read, S3-style. 국민? Your 국, 대한민국's 민. You already speak bureaucrat; you just didn't know it.

---

## Part 1 — The abstract floor

Two patterns run this vocabulary.

**Compounds go two-root.** Ring 1's concrete nouns were often single ideas; the B-list builds meaning by collision: 사회 (gather-meet = society) — flip its syllables and you get 회사, the company: the SAME two hanja, reversed. 인간 = person + between (시간's 간): to be human is to exist between others. 결과 = 결혼's tie + 과일's fruit: a result is the fruit you tied off. The radar has a growth spurt here — new 정 (politics ≠ affection), new 전 (war makes four), new 국 (결국's situation-국 ≠ country) — and hanja sorts every collision.

**The register ladder grades nouns too.** M4 taught you 엄마 vs 어머니; the same ladder runs through this module: 여자 → **여성**, 뜻 → **의미**, 요즘 → **최근**, 벌써 → **이미**(ish). Speech uses the left column; news, forms, and essays use the right. You're not learning new meanings — you're learning what they wear to work.

## Part 2 — The bricks

**The frame verbs come pre-assembled:** you'll meet 대하다/위하다/통하다/따르다 almost exclusively inside their frames — [noun]**에 대해서** (about), [noun]**을 위해서** (for the sake of), [noun]**을 통해서** (via), [source]**에 따르면** (according to). Learn the frames as chunks; the dictionary forms are just their ID cards.

## Part 3 — The glue: the quoting machine

Korean has a dedicated gearbox for reporting speech, and it runs on fuel you already own: **the plain form** — M19's 반말 dictionary style. That module wasn't just teaching you to talk to friends; it was quietly loading the quote-machine.

To report a statement, shift it to plain form, add **-고**, and hand it to a verb of saying:

| What's quoted | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| verb, present | **-ㄴ다고 / -는다고** | 온다고 해요 (they say it's coming) · 먹는다고 해요 |
| adjective, present | **-다고** (unchanged) | 바쁘다고 해요 (says he's busy) |
| anything, past | **-았/었다고** | 왔다고 해요 (says she came) |
| future | **-(으)ㄹ 거라고** | 결혼할 거라고 했어요 |
| noun (copula) | **-(이)라고** | 학생이라고 해요 |

Three notes and you own it:

1. **The future decodes itself.** 할 거예요 = badge + 거(것) + copula (M25's X-ray) — and copulas quote with -라고, so: 할 거라고. Free of charge.
2. **Swap the reporting verb, change the direction.** -다고 **해요** (they say) · -다고 **말했어요** (he said) · -다고 **들었어요** (I heard). Same quote, different courier.
3. **-(이)라고 해요 also names things.** X을/를 Y(이)라고 해요 = X is called Y — and its question is your new most-used sentence: **이거는 한국어로 뭐라고 해요?** (what's this called in Korean?). Point at things; fill your deck.

The gap deck holds the two street forms: 뭐라고요? (what did you just say?) and the hearsay contraction -대요/-래요 — the quote-machine after the 준말 reading got to it.

## Part 4 — Building sentences

**Spot the recycling:** M26's 변하다 goes through the quote-machine, M11's 비가 와요 becomes tomorrow's forecast, M31's 남산 gets its official -(이)라고 introduction, and M20's -(으)면 builds the news frame 에 따르면.

::sentences::

**Gap deck:** the one-syllable outrage 뭐라고요?, gossip's gear -대요, and how a country adopts a little sister (국민 여동생).
