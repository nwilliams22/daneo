# Korean, Word-First — Appendix S2: The Written Register

There is a version of Korean nobody speaks — and you're surrounded by it. Signs, forms, headlines, elevator buttons, exam instructions: they run on connectives too stiff for conversation, a ghost politeness level that survives only in print, and a headline grammar that throws particles overboard. This appendix teaches you to READ it, not say it — say it and you'll sound like a legal document with legs.

**How to use this:** everything here is recognition vocabulary. Drill it, spot it on your next photo of a Korean street, and never once produce it in conversation. That's not a limitation — that's fluency in knowing which register is whose.

---

## Part 1 — The stiff connectives

M29's 그러나 was your first citizen of the written register. Its colleagues:

- **및** — and (in lists): 커피 및 차 — the written 하고/와. Menus, forms, regulations
- **또는** — or: 전화 또는 문자 — where speech says -(이)나 or just pauses
- **즉** — that is / i.e.: the restater. 즉, 무료입니다 — in other words: free
- **그러므로** — therefore: 그래서 in a judge's robe. Textbook proofs end on it
- **등** — etc.: 사과, 포도, 딸기 등 — the list-closer on every receipt and syllabus

Same jobs as the spoken set (M2's 하고, M29's 그러니까), different dress code. Reading them is Ring 1 machinery; the only new skill is not flinching at the formality.

## Part 2 — The bricks

The sign-decoding kit: what doors, walls, and shutters say.

::vocab::

**The ○○ 중 system:** 중 (middle — 중국's middle-中) hangs after any noun to mean *in the middle of it*: 영업 중 (open for business), 공사 중 (under construction), 통화 중 (on the phone), 회의 중 (in a meeting), 수업 중 (in class). One syllable runs every busy-sign in the country — see the gap deck.

## Part 3 — The ghost register: 하오체

Every door in Korea gives you a command in a politeness level that died out of speech a century ago: **미시오** (push) and **당기시오** (pull) — the -시오 ending of 하오체, the old semi-formal register. It survives ONLY on signs, buttons, and public instructions:

- 미시오 / 당기시오 — push / pull (this module's 밀다/당기다, in period costume)
- -지 마시오 — the sign version of M14's -지 마세요: 수영하지 마시오
- 멈추시오 — stop (crosswalk buttons, period dramas, and nowhere else)

Hear it in sageuk dramas; read it on doors; never say it. It's the linguistic equivalent of architecture — old, load-bearing, and not conversation.

And one more written dialect: **headlines** drop particles and end in dictionary form — 김치, 세계로 가다 ("Kimchi goes global"). Bare stems, no -요, maximum compression: the newspaper's own 반말.

## Part 5 — Practice

Cover the right column and translate; then flip and build the Korean.

1. Pull the door. → 문을 당기시오.
2. No swimming here. → 이곳에서 수영하지 마시오.
3. Under construction. We apologize. → 공사 중입니다. 죄송합니다.
4. Closed on Sundays. → 일요일에는 영업하지 않습니다.
5. The entrance is on the south side. → 입구는 남쪽에 있습니다.
6. Kimchi goes global. (headline) → 김치, 세계로 가다.

Field drill: photograph five signs on your next Korean street (or street-view session) and decode them — count how many use 중, 금지, or -시오. It will be most of them.
