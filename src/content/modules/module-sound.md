# Interlude — Why Korean Sounds Different Than It Reads

A five-minute reading, no vocabulary. You've surely noticed the crime scene by now: 맛있어요 is spelled *mat-iss-eo-yo* but every romanization in this app writes **masisseoyo**. 끝나요 comes out **kkeunnayo**. 감사합니다 is **gamsahamnida**. Nobody is being sloppy — Korean has a small set of automatic sound rules, and once you know the big five, listening snaps into focus.

The comfort first: **spelling never changes.** Korean writes the *grammar* (which pieces a word is made of) and speaks the *physics* (what mouths actually do at speed). You read the spelling; your ears learn the physics.

---

## Rule 0 — Only seven sounds can end a syllable

Whatever letter sits in the 받침, only seven sounds are allowed to actually close a syllable: **ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅇ**. Every other letter collapses to its nearest neighbor when nothing follows:

- 꽃 → sounds like **꼳** (ㅊ collapses to ㄷ)
- 밖 → **박** (ㄲ → ㄱ)
- 몇 → **멷**, 옷 → **옫**, 늦다 → **늗따**

This is why 못 by itself ends in a *t*-ish sound — and it's the setup for everything below.

## Rule 1 — 연음: the batchim slides forward

When the next syllable starts with the silent ㅇ, the batchim doesn't collapse — it **moves into the empty seat**:

- 물이 → **무리** (mu-ri — this is why the app romanizes it *muri*)
- 음악을 → **으마글**
- 읽어요 → **일거요** (the buried ㄹ you met in Module 5's 읽었어요)
- 맛있어요 → **마시써요** — the ㅅ slid onto 있, the ㅆ slid onto 어

Liaison is why word-by-word reading and real speech sound like different languages. They aren't — the letters are just changing seats.

One exception: **batchim ㅇ (ng) never slides.** 고양이 (cat — coming soon) stays *go-yang-i*, not *go-ya-ngi*… well, the *ng* keeps closing its own syllable. Every other batchim jumps at the chance to move; ng alone stays seated.

## Rule 2 — Nasalization: hard stops go soft before ㄴ/ㅁ

A ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ-type batchim followed by ㄴ or ㅁ turns **nasal** (ㅇ/ㄴ/ㅁ):

- 끝나요 → **끈나요** (kkeun-na-yo — Module 8's ending, explained)
- 합니다 → **함니다** — you've been saying *hamnida* since 감사합니다, and now you know why
- 못 마셔요 → **몬 마셔요** (mon-masyeoyo)

Your mouth refuses to slam a hard stop straight into a nasal — Korean just writes the grammar and lets physics happen.

## Rule 3 — ㅎ is a trickster

- **Between vowels it vanishes:** 좋아요 → **조아요**, 많아요 → **마나요** (the "silent ㅎ" from that word's vocab note).
- **Next to ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ/ㅈ it merges into the aspirated letter:** 좋다 → **조타**, 축하해요 (congrats — coming soon) → **추카해요**.

One letter, two disappearing acts — both make words *easier* to say fast.

## Rule 4 — Tensing (the brief version)

After certain batchims, a following plain consonant tightens: 학교 sounds like **학꾜**, 식당 like **식땅**, 늦다 like **늗따**. You don't need to produce this consciously — it happens to your mouth on its own once you speak at speed. Just don't be alarmed when the audio sounds tenser than the spelling.

## Rule 5 — Plain consonants borrow their voice

Here's the one hiding in plain sight since Module 1. ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ are romanized *g d b j* — yet at the **start of a word** your ear often catches *k t p ch*: 김치 sounds closer to **Kimchi**, 부산 to **Pusan** (its old English spelling — the city is coming in Module 12). But **between vowels**, the same letters relax into true *g d b j*.

The cleanest proof is one word you've had since Module 3: **고기** (meat). Same letter ㄱ twice — but say it, or press play: **ko-gi**. First one k-ish, second one g. Nothing changed but the neighborhood.

Why: English splits *g* and *k* into different letters. Korean doesn't — its three-way split is **plain / aspirated / tense** (ㄱ/ㅋ/ㄲ), and the plain series simply has no fixed English value: crisp and voiceless-ish at the front of a word, soft and voiced mid-word. Both are the one letter ㄱ. (ㅋ, meanwhile, is *always* a hard k with a puff — that contrast is what your confusables drill trains.)

Practical upshot: don't chase the "right" English letter. Say the plain consonant lazily and let position do the work — your mouth already does this in English (*the two t's in "top" and "stop" aren't the same sound either; you've just never needed to notice*).

## What this means in practice

- **The app's romanizations already encode all of this.** They were never "wrong" — they're phonetic. When spelling and romanization disagree, the romanization is telling you the sound rule.
- **The audio button is the real teacher here.** Play any sentence and read along: every gap between letters and sound is one of the five rules above.
- **The typing drill is spelling-side.** You type 맛있어요 letter by letter (ㅁㅏㅅㅇㅣ…) even though you say *masisseoyo* — typing trains the grammar layer, listening trains the physics layer. Both are the same Korean.
- **Don't memorize the rules — recognize them.** After a week of audio-plus-reading, your ears do this automatically, the same way English speakers say "hafta" for *have to* without ever being taught.
